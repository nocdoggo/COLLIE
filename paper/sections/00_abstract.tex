\begin{abstract}
Language models can name the shock behind an operational message, but acting on what they
say is risky: a wrong reading moves real orders. We study a replenishment controller that asks a
language model only what changed, in a closed typed vocabulary, and tests the answer with an
anytime-valid e-process on data that arrive after the proposal. A preregistered pilot of the
natural design, which lets a hypothesis act only once its e-process crosses a Ville threshold,
was stopped at its own decision gate: the gate was safe but acted late, rarely, and on
magnitudes that did not match the truth. We propose certify-then-hedge. The e-process no longer
switches the controller on; it prices a fixed set of action-aligned hypotheses, the model's
answer only reweights them, the size of the shock is estimated from the data, and the order is
the critical fractile of the resulting posterior-predictive mixture. Under a known null, the
expected exposure to a false hypothesis stays within the frozen error budget at every stopping
time, whatever the model says. In an exploratory test registered before its live runs, on 240
fresh episodes with Gemini and Grok as proposers, certify-then-hedge improved net reward per
episode over the gate by 309 and 179 and over the operations research baseline by 179 and 163;
all four tests survived Holm correction, and losses on no-shock episodes stayed within the
registered margin. Nearly all of the gain over the baseline came from lead-time shifts. A
variant that ignores the model's answer, timed by the same alerts, earned as much; the model's
label cut commitment on no-shock episodes by 37 and 45 percent without changing reward. Acting
on proposals at once lost 1,478 to 3,181 in net reward per episode across six models.
\end{abstract}
