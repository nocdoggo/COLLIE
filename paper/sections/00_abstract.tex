\begin{abstract}
Language models often read operational alerts correctly, but a wrong reading moves real
orders. We study a replenishment controller that asks a language model only
what changed, in a closed typed vocabulary, and tests the answer with an anytime-valid e-process
on data that arrive after the proposal. A preregistered pilot of the natural design, which lets a
hypothesis act once its e-process crosses a Ville threshold, was stopped at its own
decision gate; with live models the gate acted late and rarely. We propose certify-then-hedge.
The e-process no longer switches the controller on but prices a fixed set of action-aligned
hypotheses, the model's answer only reweights them and dates the one it names, the size of the
shock is estimated from the data, and the order is the critical fractile of the resulting
posterior-predictive mixture. Under a known null, the expected exposure to a false hypothesis
stays within the frozen error budget, whatever the model says. In an exploratory test registered
before its live runs, on 240 fresh episodes with Gemini and Grok as proposers, the method
improved net reward per episode over the gate by 309 and 179 and over the base-stock
baseline by 178 and 163, with losses on no-shock episodes within the registered margin. Nearly all of the gain over the baseline came from
lead-time shifts, and an alert-timed variant that ignores the model's answer earned about as
much. A second registered stage confirmed the gains over the gate at the
benchmark's two other cost ratios and over the baseline at the higher one, where the model's
answer added 82 per episode with Gemini, not detectably with Grok, and no-shock losses were
not shown to stay within the margin; on noisy lead times the gain over the
baseline was not confirmed.
\end{abstract}
