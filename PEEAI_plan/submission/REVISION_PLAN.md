## 1. The core story

### 1.1 The use case

Jordan is a volunteer organizer we made up for this talk, always labelled as an illustration ("Jordan, a volunteer organizer we made up"). The event, the times, the people and the numbers are a real practice case in Plan B's sandbox (`apps/plan_b/plan_b/scenarios/private/dev01.json`, program in `PEEAI_plan/examples/campus_showcase.evaluator.json`).

1. **Setup.** A small campus showcase run by volunteers, with no operations staff. Doors open at 5 pm with a welcome. The community demonstrations at 5.10 pm need a projector, promised for 5 pm. The skills exchange follows at 5.30 pm. The skills exchange leader checks in at 5.05 pm and the demonstration presenter at 5.20 pm.
2. **The text (the moment of tension, orange).** At 4.40 pm, twenty minutes before doors open, a text says the projector "may be about 25 minutes late". If it is true, the demonstrations start with no projector. A backup projector costs $35. Jordan is carrying chairs, the advisor is not picking up, and there are three bad moves: do nothing and hope, spend $35 of club money on a maybe, or reshuffle the evening alone.
3. **Plan B checks the fact.** It treats the text as a claim, not a fact, and looks up the delivery once. In the rehearsal that look-up takes 2 minutes. The answer is "delayed, expected about 5.25 pm".
4. **Plan B keeps only the changes that fit, and the AI picks one.** Plan B lists every allowed change (keep, swap two activities, a ten-minute shift, the backup, and pairs of these), 16 in all, and throws out any that break the time, the people or the budget. 3 fit. The AI picks one of the 3, the free swap. The skills exchange moves up to 5.10 pm (its leader is there from 5.05 pm) and the demonstrations move to 5.30 pm, after the expected arrival and after the presenter checks in.
5. **Plan B checks the change and leaves a receipt.** At 4.43 pm Plan B checks the swap against the rules, makes it, and writes a receipt, a written record of what changed, when, which part of Plan B made it, and what it cost. The plan goes from version 1 to version 2. $0 spent. The $35 backup was not booked. Jordan tells the group chat and the host from the new plan on the board (Plan B sends no messages in the rehearsal).
6. **Resolution (green).** Doors open at 5 pm on time. The projector arrives at 5.24 pm, in time for the demonstrations at 5.30 pm.

**Labelled honestly.** The pictures of these beats come from a rehearsal of this case with the fixed rules (no AI). In an earlier recorded run of the same case, our AI coordinator made the same look-up and the same swap at 4.43 pm, with 3 AI calls costing under 4 cents (report 4.4, `apps/plan_b/data/recorded/wave_1/dev01_trace.json`, an earlier version `live/1.0.0`, not the tested version 1.2). Under the strip and in speech, "Acted out on a computer. In an earlier recorded run, the AI made the same choice." On a real night the look-up would be the delivery company's tracking page, and that link is not built yet.

**The 30-second version** (poster-note opener, report "Plan B in one page", the spine of deck slides 2 to 6). "Jordan, a volunteer organizer we made up, gets a text twenty minutes before doors open. The projector may be 25 minutes late. Plan B does not panic and does not spend. It checks the delivery first, which says about 5.25. It lists every possible change, keeps the 3 that fit the time, the people and the budget, and the AI picks the free one, swapping two activities. Plan B checks the change and leaves a receipt. Doors open on time, and the projector arrives in time for the demonstrations. No club money spent."

**A "what if" on the same evening (idea 3, part c).** If the look-up had said 5.45 pm, too late for either slot, Plan B holds the backup projector for free (the hold ends on its own after ten minutes) and asks the organizer to allow $35, because its own spending allowance is $0 and only a person can raise it. The organizer says OK, and only then is the backup booked, once, for $35.00. In a recorded run of this case the AI made that choice at 4.48 pm after the OK (`dev02.json`, storyboard frame `story_12_live_decision_card.png`). This is a second practice case, always labelled that way, never merged into Jordan's main run [brief, What not to claim 17].

**The pantry, only as a short second example.** A made-up campus pantry's distribution day, not based on any real pantry's data (`pantry_dev02.json`). 40 kits are needed by 1.40 pm. 24 are on the shelf and 20 are late, so the late ones are not counted until staff sign for them. Plan B holds a partner's 16 kits for free and asks staff for the handoff. Staff sign for 10 of the 16, and the other 6 stay with the partner. 34 kits are handed out on time, Plan B tells staff 6 will be short, staff decide what to tell the line, and no kit is counted twice. Two of the six final test cases were pantry days, and all 20 of their runs met the expected outcome. One spoken sentence on slide 10, one block on the poster, one backup slide, one short report section.

### 1.2 The novelty it reveals

Narrow and modest, because event platforms already check agenda changes against rules and send big changes for sign-off [1], two have announced agents whose changes a person reviews or approves [2, 3], and general assistants already ask before they buy or book [4, 5, 6]. Picking among actions already checked as possible is also a known research pattern [38, 39]. The one line, used in all three deliverables:

> Plan B puts known safety pieces together for small volunteer events. It checks the one fact a message depends on before anything changes, lets the AI pick only from changes that fit every rule, and leaves a receipt for every change, while money waits for a person. It is free, open and runs on a laptop.

Short form for the title slide, the poster header and the report's subtitle: **"Plan B checks before it changes anything."** Spoken image for the judge (slide 5): "The AI chooses. Plan B makes the change."

What the records support, and what they do not. In every approach tested, only Plan B's server can make a change, and it checks each one. In our coordinator's design the AI picks from the list of changes that fit. The tool-calling model, which calls the tools itself, met the same 18 of 18 on the six new cases, so the test does not show that the list improves outcomes. It shows the same outcomes at about a third of the cost (section 9).

### 1.3 Who benefits, in plain words

- **Student organizers.** The benefit is not the swap, which Jordan could do by hand. It is not deciding alone at 4.40 pm, not spending club money on a rumour, and having a written record ready when the treasurer or advisor asks why the program changed. The sentence a student repeats: **"It checks the text before you spend club money, and leaves the treasurer a record of why."** No user study was run, so no claim of time saved.
- **The community.** Volunteer groups and pantries face the same late deliveries and no-shows. Community events run on volunteers, and over 75.7 million people in the US volunteered through an organization between September 2022 and September 2023 [24]. The code is free to read and run, and an evening of AI calls costs cents (3 to 7 cents a run for our coordinator in the final test). Said as "can", never as "is used by".
- **Honest about the effort.** Today it needs someone comfortable running code, an account with an AI service, and new code for each new kind of day (the pantry needed a small stock extension). The pantry version handles identical boxed kits only, with no fresh food, household sizes or client choice.
- **The first group to reach.** Student clubs at UMass Boston, which has 100+ student groups [28] and a student group portal hosted on CampusGroups [14, 15]. These counts are not a market size and imply no partnership.

## 3. Words and style rules for everyone who writes

- **Writing rules (owner rule 5).** No em dash, en dash, "---", "--", semicolon or colon in any visible text, title, label, caption, script line, `\note` block or report sentence, including PDF metadata and docx core properties. Clock times read "5 pm", "5.25 pm" or "17.25" ("pm" in speech and titles, "17.25" on figure axes, "23.23 UTC" for time stamps). Web addresses without "https://" (`\href` targets may keep it). Official source titles that contain a colon print it as a comma [brief, How to cite]. Monospace command lines in the report are code and exempt.
- **Title.** "Plan B, The Show Must Go On" everywhere (`\title`, `pdftitle`, poster header, docx title and core properties, both scripts).
- **Plain names on slides and the poster.** "Plan B" is the actor that checks and makes changes (the server). The report may say "Plan B's server, the part that keeps the plan and is the only part allowed to change it" once, and speech may say it once on slide 6. "A rehearsal on a computer" for the sandbox, explained once as "a practice copy of an event on a computer, with a clock we can speed up, where nothing real is booked or bought". "Sealed before the final test" for held-out. "Looks up the delivery" for a status check. "A change" for a repair or candidate (not "fix", not "option"). "A receipt", explained the first time as "a written record of the change". "Held for free" for a hold, explained as "kept aside for ten minutes at no cost, released on its own". "Our coordinator", "the tool-calling model", "the check-all variant", "the fixed rules" in the report and backups. On slide 8 the chart groups them as "with the AI" and "rules only, no AI".
- **Never in what the audience reads or hears.** gate, arm, pipeline, orchestration layer, checkpoint, guardrail, fallback, module, harness, policy (say "approach"), executor, grant, episode, negative control, denominator, schema, API (the report's section on interfaces may say "the connections"), option id, case codes (C09, T06) in the deck and poster, receipt ids, `cand_...`, `obs_...`, `dev_fixture_01`, `rules/1.0.0`, the model id string. Product names only where cited.
- **Banned AI-sounding words** (owner list). seamless, leverage, robust, delve, harness, empower, cutting-edge, state-of-the-art, unlock, elevate, streamline, game-changer, revolutionize, transform (as hype), synergy, holistic, tapestry, landscape, navigate (figuratively), realm, paradigm, foster, unleash, supercharge, next-level, pivotal, intricate, meticulous, embark, journey (figuratively), ecosystem, innovative, powerful, effortless, smart (as hype), world-class, comprehensive (as hype).
- **Report exception.** Report sections "How Plan B works" (paths only), "How to run it" and Appendices B and C may show file paths and commands in monospace as code. Case codes may appear in one column of results tables, always beside the case's plain name.
- **The five first-year explanations.** Said once in each deliverable, in these words or close to them.
  - *A test case* is a small made-up event day written in advance, with messages that arrive at set times. We run it in the rehearsal, and at the end code checks the final state, for example that every activity happened by 6 pm, the welcome started at 5 pm, nothing started before its equipment arrived, and no rule was broken.
  - *The rehearsal on a computer* (the sandbox) is a practice copy of the event, with a clock we can speed up, where nothing real is booked or bought.
  - *A sealed case* (held out) was written and locked before the final test, and never seen by the person who changed the code. The six sealed cases use new wording but the same kinds of trouble as earlier cases.
  - *3 runs* means the same case repeated three times with the AI, because the AI can answer differently each time. The fixed rules give the same answer every time, so they ran once. 6 cases times (1 plus 3 approaches times 3 runs) is 60 runs.
  - *Met the expected outcome* means the code's checks on the final state all passed. For most cases that means the event ran on time with no rule broken. For a case that cannot be fully done, it means Plan B said so plainly instead of pretending.

## 10. What goes where, and privacy (slide 9, poster bottom block 3, report 8)

- Messages (texts, notices) come in to Plan B. The AI reads them through an outside AI service (OpenRouter, Claude Sonnet 5.5). **The plain rule, printed.** "Never put a name, address, health or immigration detail in a message Plan B reads. Every message goes to an outside AI service."
- The AI returns one choice from the list of changes that fit (our coordinator). It has no allowance to spend. A misleading message can at most lead to a choice among changes that already fit. This limits what a misleading message can do. It does not prevent prompt injection [33, 37]. Automated release checks show that text saying "ignore your instructions" is stored as content and grants nothing (release check 5), but the final test had no tricky message.
- Plan B's server is the only path to a change. It checks the role, that the plan is still current and the budget, then writes a receipt.
- Money needs a person's OK. Plan B's spending allowance is $0 and only a person can raise it. A backup can be held for free meanwhile, and if nobody answers, the hold lapses and nothing is bought.
- **Pantry dignity, printed in the report and said on the poster.** "Plan B counts kits, never people. Who gets food is always a person's call, and Plan B never decides who goes without." No recipient identities, no eligibility, allergy or medical decisions. The pantry is made up, and no pantry, U-ACCESS staff, system or partner took part.
- In this project everything is made up and local. Nothing real is booked, bought or sent.
